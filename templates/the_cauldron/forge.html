    How the Forge works — and why

    The Forge isn't a fixed routine. It measures where you are, prescribes the next honest step, and adjusts after every session. Each design choice below is anchored to the strength-science evidence — the citations are real and linked.

    1 · We test, we don't guess

    Most apps ask how hard a set felt — your "reps in reserve" (RIR). The problem: novices are unreliable at judging how close to failure they are, and tend to stop well short while believing they were near the limit. Accuracy improves with training experience, but for a beginner it's a shaky foundation.1 Trained lifters estimate effort far more accurately than novices.2 So the Forge places you with an objective AMRAP (as-many-reps-as-possible) test set per movement pattern, not a feeling — the number puts you on the right rung automatically.

    2 · Autoregulated double progression

    Your last working set each session is an AMRAP "test set." Beat the top of the rep range and you move up; fall short and you back off — the program autoregulates to your daily readiness instead of forcing a fixed weekly jump. Autoregulated progressive resistance exercise (APRE) out-performed traditional linear periodization for strength gains in a controlled trial.3

    3 · Two ways to get stronger

    When you only have bodyweight, the Forge progresses by difficulty — climbing a ladder of harder variations (wall push-up → push-up → archer push-up). When you own loadable gear, it progresses by load, adding the smallest available increment. Load mode also fixes a well-known problem: it's hard to keep overloading the lower body with bodyweight alone.

    4 · Rest like you mean it

    Each exercise carries an evidence-based rest target — roughly 2–3 minutes for heavy compounds, less for accessory and isometric work. Longer inter-set rest produced greater gains in both strength and muscle size than short rest in resistance-trained men.4

    5 · Train each pattern often

    The default Full-Body ×3 split hits every movement pattern multiple times a week. When volume is equated, higher weekly frequency tends to favour hypertrophy — and frequent practice also accelerates skill on the calisthenics movements themselves.5
